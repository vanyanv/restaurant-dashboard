Counter Pages
Counter · page library · v17

Every page, both surfaces, one system

Fifty-three screens rebuilt on Counter, reached through seventeen rail items, carrying the real menu — sliders, combos, cheese fries, shakes and Mexican Coke from Individual FoodService, Produce Direct, Sysco and US Foods. Recipes is a builder: eight ingredient lines, a live cost panel, and an uncosted line that says so rather than quietly rounding down. Everything is still here — the detail pages moved behind the rows that open them, seven pages became view tabs on the page they belong to, and two hubs that only relinked were deleted. Two controls above the stage put any page into any of its five states: live, loading, one section failed, empty, stale.

The date range is the most-used control in the product

Every figure on every page is a claim about a window of time, so the window sits in the page head on all of them, always right-anchored: steppers either side to walk back a period at a time, twelve presets, a calendar for anything else, and the comparison the numbers are judged against — prior period, the same four weekdays, or the same days last year. The calendar underlines the comparison days in yellow so you can see what you are being measured against. It stays inside its frame at every width: right-aligned on a desk, clamped when the column is tight, and a full-width bottom sheet with a scrim under 640px, where a 438px popover would otherwise hang off the screen.

Where the data comes from

Every name on these screens is taken from the repository, not invented. The menu is sliders and combos because that is what the fixtures say you sell; the beef is 73/27 Creekstone, the rolls are Martin’s potato rolls, the packaging is a branded t-shirt bag and a #50 paper tray, and the vendor whose invoice will not reconcile is Individual FoodService. The categories are the ones in OtterMenuCategory. The quantities are representative rather than live — deterministic, internally consistent, and never real revenue on a page that can be shared.

What the rail is for

A rail item is a place you decide to go. Everything else is somewhere you end up. That one rule removed half the nav: nine detail routes now open from the row that names them, the store switcher replaced every [storeId] page, and two hub pages whose only content was links to their own neighbours are gone. Seven more pages became view tabs on the page they were always part of — Product usage was never a peer of Vendors, it is the other half of COGS.

The five states, on every page

Every screen here was drawn at full data, on a good day. The app is not like that: it has 20 loading files, 9 error boundaries, roughly 20 “X unavailable” section failures and 16 distinct empty messages. The state switch above the stage applies to all fifty-three pages at once, because the states live in the shared builders rather than in each page. Pick Glendale in the rail and the empty state arrives on its own — a pre-open store has no data, which is the same thing.

Where the wordmark goes

The logo is red and yellow, which is where Counter’s accent and signal came from in the first place. It runs full size on login, sits at the head of the rail on every desktop page, and appears once on the phone — in More, where the account lives. It stays off the phone header, because at 20px tall a wide cartoon wordmark is a smudge and the store name is the thing you actually need there.

What is drafted, and what is still owed

Fifty-three screens and five states each. A route-by-route audit against the app found eight surfaces this library did not have — five monitoring tabs and three per-store deep dives — and they are drafted here, as views on the pages that own them rather than as new rail items. Several existing pages gained the section the app has and this did not, and Ask stopped being a page you go to: it opens over whatever you are on. Every route below exists in the app today, every field on the store file matches the schema, and the two exceptions are named honestly: an ingredient detail and a vendor detail are new routes I am proposing, not ones you have.

The rail, after — 17 items

  • Today · Overview, Ask, Needs you, Orders
  • Money · Analytics, P&L, COGS, Labor
  • Menu · Menu, Recipes
  • Stock and suppliers · Invoices, Inventory, Ingredients, Vendors
  • Admin · Stores, Settings, Monitoring

The eight that were missing

  • Monitoring runs eight tabs, not three. Infra, Costs, Cache, Activity and the ingredient audit are drafted — token health, table sizes and retention, AI spend, hit rate by prefix, the sync grid, and the auto-match ladder with its revert rate.
  • Analytics, Labor and COGS each have a per-store page. Most of it is the group page filtered, but not the day book, the statement or the category table — those have no group form. They live as a second view on the parent, and P&L now matches.
  • The rail is still seventeen items. A monitoring tab is not a destination, and a per-store page is what the store switcher is for.

Ask, anywhere

  • ⌘K opens it over whatever you are on, and it says what it is answering about before you type — the page, the store, the range.
  • Every section head’s Ask about this now works. It was rendered on fifty pages and wired on none.
  • The answer appears in the surface, with what it read, the arithmetic and where to go next. Escape backs out of the answer; a second Escape closes it.
  • Everything the rail reaches, it reaches: pages, the views inside them, the store switcher and the twelve date presets.
  • The Ask page keeps what the surface answers — a thread with its history, the cost and sources on each turn, and a fork.

Sections the app had and this did not

  • Menu profit gained the honesty strip: how much revenue the margins above did not see, because an unmapped item costs nothing until a recipe is mapped to it.
  • Needs you filters on both axes — three severities and the five sources that actually raise an alert, named as they are stored.
  • Inventory gained the adjustment, which is how a variance reaches shrink instead of silently reaching food cost.
  • Invoices gained top products, COGS the worst-margin items, Labor the twelve-week trend, and Product usage the two segments it had only labelled.
  • The store file became editable, and Settings gained the team block, because there is an invite record and nothing that issues one.

Three stages, not two

  • The model has pre-open, warming up and trading, and only two were expressible here. The rail tag, the store file’s lifecycle row and the ask surface handle the middle one now, and a warming store carries a caption naming whose shape its forecast came from.
  • The demo data stays one trading and two opening. Four notes in the log and the whole empty state are written around that; flipping a store mid-flight would have left the prototype contradicting its own record.
  • warming: true on a store in STORES is the whole switch.

What moved, and why

  • Alerts + Decisions → Needs you. Two queues asking the same question at two speeds: what needs me now, and what needs me this week.
  • Catalog + Menu profit + Product mix → Menu. Three views of one menu: what it is, what it earns, what sells.
  • COGS + Product usage. Theoretical against actual is the second half of a cost page, not a page.
  • Inventory + Stock counts + Start a count. One page: what is on hand, what the counts found, and the button that starts the next one.
  • Ingredients + Price monitor, Vendors + Packaging, Monitoring + Model + People.
  • Demoted, not deleted: the Menu hub is the Menu section’s first tab, and the Operations hub is where the phone’s More tab and the deeper breadcrumbs land. A hub is a tax as a rail item and useful as a landing.

The store file earns its own page

  • Rent, fixed labor, towels, cleaning — the four inputs the P&L expects, each with its own cadence.
  • Six fixed expenses that become their own P&L rows, exactly as StoreFixedExpense does today.
  • Commission rates and the COGS target, because a wrong Uber rate makes every third-party margin wrong.
  • The arithmetic is shown: monthly × 12 ÷ 365 × days in range. Change the range and it recalculates in front of you.

How the pages connect

  • A row opens its record. Invoices to an invoice, orders to an order, the catalogue to an item, the counts to a count, the ingredient list to an ingredient, the vendor list to a vendor — on the desk and on the phone.
  • The route is the hierarchy. /dashboard/invoices/I28517 makes Invoices the parent, which is where the breadcrumb and the phone’s back button come from. Nothing is hand-wired.
  • A tab is a destination. A link to Catalog opens Menu on its Items tab, so a page that lives in a tab strip is never shown without one.
  • Five pages are deliberately unreachable — login, invite, maintenance, 404, 403. You arrive at them from outside the app.

What the P&L does now

  • Prime cost is the headline — food plus labour against the 60% ceiling on the store file, because it is the one line with a published benchmark and an action threshold behind it.
  • A cascade, not a donut. The bar is what is left after each subtraction; only a line over its own target is coloured.
  • Eight weeks you can press. Each row is the same statement over that week, and pressing it moves the date control.
  • Fixed costs prorate and the page says so; the comparison is always the same number of days.
  • A trust panel: what was measured, what was prorated, what is a rate rather than a bill, and what three unposted invoices would do to the bottom line.

What the date control does

  • Twelve presets, each showing its own length, plus steppers that walk back by exactly the range you are on.
  • Buckets follow the span: days up to a month, weeks up to four months, months beyond.
  • The comparison is part of the range, not a separate setting, and it appears in every chart tooltip.
  • A single day unlocks single-day panels — the model's call and Still moving. Widen the range and they are replaced rather than faked.

What the deep dive changed

  • Service by the hour is gone from Overview, replaced by sales per labour hour with the $68.00 floor drawn on it. An hourly chart could not answer a thirty-day range.
  • The masthead dispatch line is back — needs-you, trading, and whether the figures can be trusted, in one line.
  • The per-store ledger that the financial-summary section produces, one card a store.
  • Labor gained a verdict, a day-by-day week, the staffing curve and the leak ledger — 6.2 hours and $142 of early clock-ins and late clock-outs.
  • Ingredients gained the review inbox and the modifier mapping table; Inventory gained coverage health; Invoices gained the spend trend.

The numbers agree with each other

  • Blended margin is 68.6% because food cost is 31.4%. One is the other subtracted from a hundred, and both are generated from the same table.
  • The catalog, the ledger, the quadrant and the phone list all render from a single menu array, so a price cannot disagree with a margin.
  • A Double Slider costs $2.27 — a quarter pound of 73/27, a potato roll, two slices of cheese, half an ounce of house sauce, onion, pickle, a tray and half a bag.
  • Fixed costs prorate from $13,572 a month to $446.20 a day to whatever the range is — and the P&L charges that, not a whole month.

Motion

  • One orchestrated entry per screen — sections rise in reading order, 36ms apart, finished inside a third of a second.
  • Charts draw themselves once. Lines stroke on over 720ms, bars grow from the baseline 26ms apart, meters and cost bars fill from the left.
  • Figures count up to what they already say, over 480ms, keeping their currency and their decimals.
  • Every consequential button answers back with a toast — Saved, Committed, Approved and posted to COGS.
  • One thing loops: the dot beside “3 need you”, and only while something needs you.
  • All of it off under prefers-reduced-motion, counters and toasts included.

The states kit

  • Loading — the shape of the page in skeletons, so you know what is coming before the figures land.
  • One section failed — a named failure with a retry, and the rest of the page untouched. That is how the app already behaves.
  • Empty — different copy for a pre-open store and for a filter that matched nothing, because they need different next steps.
  • Stale — the sync failed four hours ago, the dot turns red, and a banner says the figures below are the last good run.

What a detail route owes you

  • The whole chain, not a summary. An order goes ticket → commission → tax → net → food cost → contribution, and names the modifier that makes the contribution optimistic.
  • The evidence beside the extraction. The invoice shows the scan and the parsed lines together, with the two flagged rows tinted in both.
  • What it touches. An ingredient lists the SKUs matched to it, their pack conversions, and the six recipes it sits in with the margin each one lost.
  • A gate, where a gate belongs. Approve stays disabled until the gap is zero, and says so in words.

How the charts behave

  • Hover anywhere on a chart, not just on a point. The nearest reading wins.
  • A crosshair, a dot, and a card naming the day, every series, and the comparison it is being judged against.
  • Bars dim to 42% except the one under the pointer, so the reading is unambiguous.
  • Touch works the same way — drag across a chart on the phone and the card follows.

Rules carried forward

  • Bricolage on page titles and the wordmark only; every figure is DM Sans, tabular.
  • No card grid for numbers — one lead figure, one ruled strip.
  • Tables get horizontal rules only, sticky headers, right-aligned figures.
  • Every screen ends in a decision. If a page has nothing to act on, it says so.
The log

Fifty-one decisions, and what each one cost to find

Numbered in the order they were found, not the order they are read, and stable so the notes can point at each other. The count starts at 15 because the first fourteen belong to cuts that no longer exist.

15

The wordmark is the palette’s alibi

Counter’s red and yellow read as a designer’s choice until you put the logo next to them, at which point they read as the brand. That is worth more than a neutral shell, so the mark gets real estate on login and permanent residence at the top of the rail.

16

A chart you cannot interrogate is a picture

Every series carries its own reading on hover, with the comparison baked into the card — not just “$5,871” but “$5,871, and the four-Friday band was $5,284 to $6,458”. The tooltip is where the pace line lives once the chart is interactive.

17

Both devices move together

Picking a page switches the desk and the phone at once. Designing them side by side is the only way to notice that a five-column table has no phone answer until you give it one.

29

The labour figures were wrong by two and a half times

Decisions gained a day-detail panel that divides a day’s forecast by its planned hours, and it printed $164 an hour against a headline claiming $58.40. Two hundred and fourteen hours cannot produce $34,524. It is 480 hours at $17.84 loaded, which is $8,562, which is the 24.8% the P&L already said. Putting one derived figure on screen exposed three pages of numbers that had never been multiplied out.

28

Three bugs the triple-check found

A guard compared eff() against 'ok' when that function returns 'live', so Still moving never rendered. Every breakpoint inside the app frame was a viewport media query, and the frame is 900px inside a 1500px page, so none of them ever fired — they are container queries now. And a one-day range drew a chart with a single bar, which fills the panel edge to edge and says nothing; it is a reading instead.

26

Placeholder names hide broken arithmetic

Swapping invented items for the real menu immediately exposed rows where a price and a margin disagreed — a $6.50 shake carrying a $4.55 plate cost and still claiming 68%. Generating every table from one array fixed it permanently, and it is the reason a spec is worth building before the code.

27

Motion earns its place by being brief

The entry animation exists to show the shape of the page arriving in reading order, which is what streaming actually does here. It finishes in 330ms. Anything longer is decoration, and decoration on a page someone opens twelve times a day becomes an obstacle.

24

A rail item is a decision, not an inventory

Thirty-two items is not navigation, it is a table of contents. Seventeen fits in one glance without scrolling, which matters because the rail is the only part of the product visible on every screen. Nothing was removed — it moved to where you were already standing when you wanted it.

25

The store switcher deletes a whole class of route

Every /[storeId] page — analytics, P&L, COGS, labor — existed because there was no other way to scope a page to one store. With a switcher in the rail, they are the same page with a different scope, and four nav items stop existing.

22

States belong in the builders, not the pages

Loading, failed, empty and stale are implemented once inside sec(), strip() and chart(). That is why all fifty-three pages have all five states rather than the four screens somebody remembered. Designing them per page is how half a product ends up with a spinner and the other half with nothing.

23

A pre-open store is an empty state, not a special case

Glendale has no sales because it has no customers. Rather than a bespoke screen, picking it in the rail puts every page into empty — with copy that says what it is waiting for and a link to the one thing that is actionable: the store file, where rent is still missing.

21

A popover that leaves its frame is broken, not clever

The range picker is 438px wide, which is wider than a phone. It now measures its own frame on open: right-anchored when there is room, clamped when there is not, and a full-width bottom sheet with a scrim below 640px. The trigger sheds its Today button and its comparison line first, so the label survives longest.

19

A range that only changes the label is a lie

Picking a range regenerates the series, the totals, the bucket size and the tooltips. It was faster to fake, and faking it would have hidden the real question: what does this page look like over ninety days, when a fourteen-point line becomes thirteen weekly bars?

20

Fixed costs prorate, and the page shows its working

Rent is monthly; a seven-day range is not a month. The store file prints the arithmetic rather than a number, because an owner who cannot see how a month of fixed cost became a week of it has no reason to trust the prime cost on the Overview.

18

Login is a product surface, not a gate

It is the only screen every user sees, and the only one that is pure brand. It carries the wordmark at full size, one sentence about what the tool is for, and the last sync figures, so the app looks alive before anyone is inside it.

30

An owner checks two numbers, so the lead carries two

Net sales says whether the day happened. Sales per labour hour says whether it was worth having. The second one was a cell in the strip and a chart four sections down, which is where you put a number you want people to miss. It is a lead figure now, with the floor drawn under it, and the strip picked up prime cost in the space it left.

31

The same table twice is the same table nobody reads

Overview drew every store side by side at the top and again as the per-store ledger at the bottom — seven columns against eight, the same three rows. The prime-cost panel was a third printing of food cost and labour from the strip. One ledger survives, prime cost moved into the strip beside the two figures it is made of, and nothing was lost but the repetition.

32

Where the money came from belongs to the store that made it

“Where today came from” sat in a panel of its own, four channels with no store attached — fine with one location, meaningless with three. It is a drawer under the ledger row now: open Hollywood and you get its channels, its commission on each, and what it keeps. The drawer is generated from the row's own total, so the parts always add to the whole above them. A pre-open store opens onto the reason it has none.

34

Seven figures answered in prose, one in a picture

Every cell in the strip asks the same thing — is this number where it should be? — and answered it with the reference printed as text, leaving the reader to subtract. Two of the six were in breach and nothing said so: avg ticket at $24.61 under a band starting at $25.10, food cost 2.4 points over plan. They all take the mark now, and the floor meter under sales per labour hour stopped being a one-off and became the same component at lead scale. Eighteen pixels a row.

35

Colour the overshoot, not the measure

The first cut painted the whole measure bar red on a breach, which reads as a lot of bad rather than past the line. Only the distance between the reference and the figure is coloured now, so the red on food cost is literally the 2.4 points. Three states, and the third — amber, the last tenth before the edge — is there because marketplace fees at $684 against a band ending at $690 is technically fine and practically a warning.

36

The channel colours cannot carry the data

Run the four brand hexes through a CVD check and they fail as a set: DoorDash and Grubhub are ΔE 8.5 apart in normal vision, and In-house and Uber Eats have chroma under 0.01, which is to say they are greys. So identity stays on the chip beside the name, where a label is always present, and the bar does the measuring: its length is the channel’s share of net, solid is what the store keeps and hatched is what the marketplace takes. The fee was the most decision-relevant number in that drawer and it had no form at all.

33

The chart sat under a floor its own headline denied

Promoting the figure exposed it. The headline said $71.93 an hour against a $68.00 floor; the chart beside it generated 68 labour hours against $4,600 of sales, which is $67.65 — under the floor, every day, while the text above called it comfortable. Two other pages said the floor was $52.10. Hours are derived from sales and the hour's own return now, the lead figure is the series' own average rather than a literal, and $68.00 is the only floor on the page: $17.84 loaded at 26.2% of net, which is the top of the labour band the strip already prints.

37

The comparison was drawn three times and measured once

A ribbon behind the chart, a delta on six figures, and a six-row table of its own — three encodings of one relationship, and the loudest of them was fake. The ribbon was this period’s net plus or minus ten percent, which compares the range to itself and then labels the result a 4-week band. It is a dashed reference line now, drawn from a series with its own shape; the strip’s deltas are the second read; and every figure at once is a drawer under the chart, which is where note 16 said the comparison belonged. Set the comparison to None and all of it disappears, because a page that prints a change with no comparison is lying twice. Note 54 finished the job: the percentage itself was still typed, and is now the difference between two real windows.

38

Two of the three rows were em-dashes

A table asks every row to answer the same columns. Glendale and Van Nuys have no sales, no orders, no ticket, no labour, no food cost and no prime, so the ledger printed twelve em-dashes and called it a store list. They are cards now, and the two that are not trading show the figure they do have — build-out at 68% and 31%, what signed off, what is resubmitted, and the rent still missing from the store file. The group row went with the table: with one store trading it was Hollywood printed a second time.

39

A total is the sum of the series drawn beside it

Promoting a figure exposes it, the same way note 33 went. Set the range to a single day and the chart read $72.61 an hour while the sentence directly under it read $72.57 — the total was a parallel sum over the same days, the chart was the rounded series, and four cents of disagreement is still disagreement. Net sales, orders and hours all come off their own series now, so a one-day range states one number twice. The other half of that fix: one reading is not a six-column grid. The strip fallback went back to flex, because four empty tracks beside a figure read as a hole where data should be.

40

Four dollar lines cannot show a mix shift

Analytics said so in its own note — four channels on one axis, so a mix shift is visible rather than inferred — over a chart of four dollar lines, which show levels. A mix shift is a share moving, and the only chart that shows one has share on its axis. It is a hundred-percent stacked area now, and the drill under it puts a number on the move: the first third of the range against the last. There was also no shift in the data to show. The four channels were four independent series that on the worst day summed to 3.8% more than the net they were shares of, and DoorDash drifted −0.2 points across the range while Ask and Overview both told you it was up four. They are shares of one net now, so the parts add to the whole in every bucket, and the drift is the one the rest of the library already claims.

41

The bands are separated by lightness, and named on themselves

The four brand hexes were carrying the whole chart. Run them through a CVD check and DoorDash and Grubhub are ΔE 8.5 apart in normal vision — the floor is 15 — while In-house and Uber Eats have chroma under 0.01, which is to say they are two greys. The stacked bands use an ordered warm ramp instead: every adjacent pair clears ΔE 15 in normal vision and under all three CVD models, a 2px surface rule keeps two fills from ever touching, and the channel name is written on its own band. Colour is the third encoding, so the ramp could fail entirely and the chart would still read. The brand hex stays where it is a label and not a measure — the chips on the order list and in the store drawer.

42

Three things a close look at one page found underneath it

A ratio does not have a busy Saturday. The generator multiplied every series by the weekday weight, including the ones that are percentages, so food cost swung from 24.8% on a Tuesday to 44.7% on a Saturday and the 29.0% plan line meant nothing. Every long range opened on a zero. A part-week bucket was anchored to the Monday before the range and then walked seven days forward from there, so it read days that were not in the range and came back empty — which is why 90 days showed DoorDash at 20.5% in its first third. And four cells in a six-column grid left two grey holes: the strip drew its hairlines as a ruled background behind a 1px gap, which also paints every track a short row leaves empty. Each cell rules its own edges now.

43

The one page that answered for a store you were not looking at

Ask is the longest-held page in the product — a 3m 31s median against 1m 12s on Overview — and it was the only one of the forty-five with no states, no store and no range. Fourteen hardcoded labels and a literal array of fourteen percentages, so the header could read reading Aug 15–21 over a chart of Aug 8–21, and Glendale — which has never sold anything — got a confident explanation of its food cost. Every figure is read off the two controls now, and the states finally mean something specific on an answer surface: loading is the reading, named source by source, because an answer is worth exactly what it read; empty says a pre-open store has no food cost to explain and offers the two questions it can answer; a source that does not come back is struck through in the provenance rather than quietly dropped.

44

A caveat in a different unit is a caveat nobody can weigh

The old page listed its sources as four identical pills, and the fourth one said excluded 3 unposted invoices in the same weight as read Otter daily sales. An exclusion is not a source; it is the reason the answer might be wrong. It has its own panel now, and it is stated in the unit of the answer: three invoices, $1,290 of food lines, $301 of it inside this range — 0.9 points, against a channel mix that explains 0.8. The biggest single uncertainty in the answer is larger than its second-biggest cause, so the page says the cheapest way to improve the answer is to post them, and puts that before the price change.

45

A gap is a part-to-whole, and it had four KPI cells

2.4 points decomposed into three causes is the one place in the library where four figures are parts of one thing, and it used the component built for figures that are not. The gap is a bar now: the bar is the overshoot, its segments are what made it, the three are named on the row under it, and the arithmetic is printed so it can be checked by eye. The chart below is the same three causes bucket by bucket off the same zero — which is the plan — so one decomposition serves both scales and there is no second colour scheme. The band labels carry names and not values, because a band's last bucket is not the range average printed beside it, and two numbers under one label is how a page starts disagreeing with itself. Underneath, food cost stopped being a series with a plan drawn near it and became the plan plus the things that moved it — which is why widening the range past the three-week price rise correctly reports that ground beef is not the answer.

46

Two surfaces promised ⌘K and nothing opened

The topbar button and the Overview ask bar both printed the shortcut, the palette was fully designed — input, group headings, selected row, footer hints — and it was mounted nowhere. Fourteen rules of dead CSS behind an advertised shortcut, which is worse than never mentioning it. It opens now, inside the frame rather than over the page, filters as you type, moves on the arrow keys and switches store on Enter. The composer was the same kind of lie: a div with a placeholder-coloured span, on three separate surfaces. They are inputs.

47

Two hundred and forty-eight rows wore a pointer and led nowhere

.tbl tbody tr set cursor:pointer and an accent hover wash on every row of every table in the library, and not one of them opened anything. The same promise made 248 times and kept none, plus 138 phone rows with a chevron typed into the value column for the same reason. A cursor is a claim about what a click does. The cursor, the wash, the focus rail and the chevron now belong to tr[data-goto] and arrive with the destination or not at all — and because a row is not a button, it carries role="link", a tab stop, and an Enter/Space handler.

48

Fourteen pages had no way in, and the route strings already knew the way

Six detail pages — an order, an invoice, a menu item, a count, an ingredient, a vendor — existed in full and could only be reached by picking them out of the index below. Meanwhile every page carried a route like /dashboard/invoices/I28517, a complete hierarchy that nothing read. It is read now: the parent of a page is the deepest route above it, which gives the breadcrumb its path, the phone its back button, and the crawler something to check. Twenty-one buttons that said Open invoices, Open the count, Open catalog now open them. Five pages are still unreachable, and that is correct: you arrive at a login, an invite, a maintenance screen, a 404 and a 403 from outside the app, so the index groups them under Entered from outside rather than leaving them looking dropped.

49

One item, six different items

The Double Slider was a $2.27 plate at 74.6% on the recipe pages and a $3.71 plate at 61% on the catalogue pages — filed under Sliders in one place and Burgers in another, with seven recipe lines on one screen and eight on the next. Only one set could be checked against anything: MENU holds 612 sold at $8.95 on a $2.27 plate, and 612 × $6.68 is the $4,088 the recipe page already printed. Every page that names the item now reads that row. The ingredient page was worse than inconsistent: it costed the Double Slider at a third of a pound against the recipe’s quarter, and listed a milkshake among the six recipes containing ground beef. It is the same six Ask answers with, from the same array, and the pounds are the recipe’s.

50

Four commission rates for one DoorDash

CHAN has held the marketplace rates since the first cut — DoorDash 25%, Uber Eats 23%, Grubhub 20% — and the blended 23.3% on the Orders strip is computed from it. Everything else was typed: an order billed 20%, a menu item billed 20%, 21% and 17%, and Ask described a 15% markup rule while the item priced at 27%. One fee table and one markup rule now price every channel on every page. Fixing it exposed a second thing: the order page listed − tax, remitted by the platform as a subtraction and then printed a net that had not subtracted it. Tax collected by DoorDash was never yours, so it is stated beside the arithmetic instead of pretending to be part of it.

51

The P&L had a date control and thirteen ranges, and one set of numbers

It printed a date range in its header, offered every preset the rest of the library offers, and showed June, July and August to date whatever you picked. Thirteen ranges, one figure set — so the header read Aug 15–21 over a table of three months. Worse, the comparison inside that table was a partial August against a whole July and called the difference growth: twenty-one days beside thirty-one, with a full month of rent charged to both. Every line is now the sum of a series the library already draws, the comparison window is the same number of days by construction, and fixed costs prorate — monthly × 12 ÷ 365 × days, the arithmetic the store file already showed and the P&L ignored.

52

A statement is a sequence of subtractions, so it is drawn as one

The old page answered where does the revenue go with a five-slice donut, which answers what share — a different question, and the one a percent column already answers better. It is a cascade now: the bar is what is left after each line, the slice beside it is what that line took, and you watch it shrink down the page to the bottom line. Colour marks the exception and nothing else, so five cost rows are not five red bars — only a line over its own target is coloured. Prime cost is the subtotal the trade actually manages to, and it was not on the statement at all; food-plus-labour against the 60% ceiling from the store file is now the second thing on the page.

53

Eight weeks, and pressing one is the same act as picking it

Weekly is the cadence the trade runs on: a prime-cost variance found in week one can be fixed in week two, and the same variance found in a monthly close has already run for four weeks. The eight-week list is the same statement computed over eight windows — not a summary of it — so a row promises exactly what the page shows when you press it, and pressing it moves the control at the top of the frame. The list anchors on today rather than on the range, because a list that re-bases when you press a row slides out from under you. The current week is drawn short and labelled 5 of 7 days: its dollars are smaller for that reason alone, and the rates beside them are not.

54

▲ 8.4%, on five pages, for every range and every comparison

The comparison control offers four choices — prior period, the same four weekdays, last year, none — and the figure beside it was the constant CMP_UP = 1.084 typed into five pages. Switch to last year, widen to ninety days, it still said 8.4%. The comparison is a real window now, read with the same generators over different dates: the prior n days, the mean of the four preceding same-weekday windows, or the same days last year. One function returns the arrow, the figure and the comparison's name, and the P&L runs the whole statement over that window so the change column is points of sales against the same number of days. Two neighbours came with it: the store file's own lines summed to $13,572 a month while every page printed $12,940, and its Uber rate said 21% against the 23% the order pages bill.

55

Fifty section heads offered to answer a question and none of them could

The Ask about this button was rendered by the section builder, styled down to its hover state, and bound to nothing — the same defect as note 46, fifty times over. The fix was to make the button carry its own question: the builder’s fourth argument used to be a boolean meaning “show the button” and is now the question it asks, with the old true falling back to the section’s title. A palette that only answers what it was pre-loaded with is a demo, so the first row of the list is always the thing you actually typed, and a question it cannot answer says what it can see — this store, this range, these four sources — rather than an apology.

56

Five monitoring tabs were missing and one of them was the one that caught a live bug

The app runs eight; this drew three. Costs is the one worth naming: every chat turn had been recording $0 for two days, and a spend chart draws a day of zero exactly like a quiet weekend. So the drafted page counts it — turns recording $0 is a figure in the strip — and the flat bars are drawn in the warning colour rather than vanishing into the baseline. Cache is ranked by misses rather than by rate, because a blended 96.2% hides one cold prefix behind six warm ones, and the cold one is half of every miss on the system. Activity opens with a sentence, because seven panels is too many to read to find out whether anything is wrong.

57

The store switcher deletes the route, but not the sections

The argument for having no per-store pages was that picking a store filters the group page. That is true of most of it and false of three sections: the day book, the statement and the category table have no group form, because a day book across three stores is three day books. So the route survives as the second view on its parent, reached from the switcher rather than from the rail. All four Money pages now work the same way — previously the per-store P&L existed and was reachable from nowhere.

58

The middle lifecycle stage existed in the model and nowhere in the interface

The model has three stages — pre-open skips training entirely, warming up borrows Hollywood’s shape because the store is open but has too little history of its own, and trading runs a native model. Only two were expressible here: a store was either producing figures or producing an empty state. The rail tag, the store file’s lifecycle row and the ask surface all handle the middle one now, and a warming store carries a caption saying whose shape its forecast came from. The demo data deliberately stays one trading and two opening: four notes in this log and the whole empty state are written around that, and a prototype that contradicts its own record is worse than one that demonstrates one stage fewer. Setting warming: true on a store in STORES is the whole switch.

59

One class name, two components, and the newer one won

.wk was the seven-day grid on Labor and Decisions. The P&L’s week table was given the same name, and because .wk .bar and .wkd .bar carry identical specificity, the later rule won: the day grid’s 4px progress bars were rendering 14px tall with a 70px minimum width and an absolutely-positioned fill, on two pages that had nothing to do with the change. Nothing errored and no probe caught it, because the markup was still correct — only the cascade was wrong. Found by listing every selector defined twice with no property in common, then counting the elements matched by both rules in a rendered page. The table is .wkt now.

60

Prime cost had two definitions and they were 1.7 points apart

Overview read 56.2% and the P&L read 57.9% for the same range, because one counted hourly wages and the other counted hourly plus the salaried line from the store file. Both cells were labelled Labor and both figures were called Prime cost. It is the one number in the product with a published ceiling behind it, so it cannot be two numbers. Prime is the whole wage bill everywhere now; the schedule’s own share stays on the Labor page as Hourly labor, with the salaried figure beside it. The same sweep caught the Labor page typing 24.8% while the P&L computed it, Decisions printing the plan $71.93 as though it were the actual, and the per-store Labor page dividing sales by a 68.4 that exists nowhere else — 505 hours against the 480 the group page reads.

61

Two pages were still reading figures the rest of the library had left behind

Menu profit printed Revenue $141,200 and Food cost $44,340 under a header showing the selected range — the exact monthly pair the P&L was rebuilt to stop printing. Vendors was worse in a quieter way: its strip said $10,703 for the range and the table directly beneath it summed to $18,420, a thirty-day set, with the bar chart agreeing with the table and not the strip. Both read the range now, and a vendor’s own page reads the same share of the same total as the row that opens it. Found by rendering all fifty-three pages and grouping every strip figure by its label: a label with more than one value is either a contradiction or a label doing two jobs.

62

Five and a half kilobytes of styling for components that no longer exist

Forty-seven class names — a detail side panel, a service-by-the-hour chart, an analytics heat grid, invoice confidence bars, three generations of phone scaffolding — outlived the screens that used them. Proving it took two independent passes: render every page in every state with every tab and drawer open and collect the classes that actually appear, then search the source for each name being emitted at all, including through className and string concatenation. Only names failing both tests were removed, and the rendered class list is byte-identical afterwards. A fossil went with them: NET14, a hardcoded fourteen-day sales array from before the date control existed, still referenced by three helpers that referenced nothing else.

63

The review surface had a picture of an invoice, not the invoice

The page carried a hand-typed mock of six lines and three dead spans labelled Prev, Next and Open PDF. Meanwhile the product already stores the real thing: the attachment arrives by email, goes into private object storage, and is served through a signed, no-store route. It is the only copy that proves what a line said. The document is now half the page, with real paging, real zoom, and the stored object named underneath — file, size, bucket, upload time — because “check it against the PDF” is not advice you can act on unless the PDF is there.

64

The extraction has no coordinates, so nothing pretends to have them

The obvious design is to highlight a region of the page when you touch a row. The stored extraction has vendor, SKU, quantity, unit, price and a line number — and no geometry at all. Drawing a box would be inventing data. The tie is the printed line number: a gutter down the document, a column in the table, and pressing either one selects both, turning to the page it is printed on. It is a weaker link than a bounding box and it is a true one, and it degrades honestly — a line that cannot be found is a line that was never numbered.

65

The failure worth designing for is a line that is missing

A table of extracted lines can show you a wrong number. It cannot show you an absent one — and that is the failure this invoice actually has. The document prints nineteen lines totalling $1,341.96; the extraction holds eighteen totalling $1,284.19; the $57.77 between them is a line of slider seasoning that the model dropped. Both totals and the gap are summed from the same array, so they cannot drift. The extracted table keeps the missing row visible as a hole rather than closing over it, the document marks it in the margin, and the consequence is named: it is the one ingredient the recipe page cannot cost, because no invoice line ever created it. The list page gained the column that matters for the same reason — three of thirty-four invoices have no file at all, synced before storage existed, and an extraction with nothing behind it cannot be checked by anyone.
